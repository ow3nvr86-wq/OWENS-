THE DANCING PLAGUE OF 1518 - RESEARCH AND SOURCES
==================================================

What this file is: the research behind every spoken line and every piece of on-screen text in the Short,
which sources support it, where the sources disagree, and what was left out or shown only as an artistic
reconstruction. The narration avoids anything that only one weak source supports.

What the primary record is: the 1518 events are known from Strasbourg council decisions and from
chronicles. Some chronicles were written decades later, and Paracelsus's account, which names the first
dancer, dates from about 1532 ([WIKI], [PDR]). The details below are therefore "reported", not certain.


2. VERIFIED TIMELINE
--------------------
July 1518      A woman begins dancing in a Strasbourg street. Later accounts name her Frau Troffea; some
               give 14 July. She is reported to have danced for four to six days ([WALE], [PDR], [BRIT], [HIST]).
Within a week  About 34 others had joined, according to Waller ([WALE]); "about three dozen" ([HIST]);
               "more than thirty" within days ([PDR]).
By August      Estimates run from about 50 to about 400 people. The high figure comes from one chronicle,
               the Imlin family chronicle, which says 400 within a month ([WIKI], [PDR], [WALE]).
July-August    The council consults physicians. After ruling out astrological and supernatural causes, they
               call it a "natural disease" caused by "hot blood" or "overheated blood" ([WALE], [PDR], [BRIT]).
               Their remedy is more dancing.
               The council clears two guildhalls and the outdoor grain market and builds a wooden stage,
               opposite the horse fair according to [WALE] (in the horse market according to [PDR]). It pays
               musicians and dancers to keep the afflicted moving ([WALE], [PDR], [HIST]).
Then           The approach seems to make things worse. The council pulls down the stages and bans public
               dancing and music; exceptions are later reported for stringed instruments at weddings
               ([PDR], [WIKI]).
August         Dancers, beginning with the worst affected, are taken to the shrine of Saint Vitus at Saverne,
               in the hills north-west of Strasbourg. The rituals reportedly included red shoes, holy water,
               crosses and prayer ([PDR], [WIKI], [HIST]: "a mountaintop shrine").
Early Sept.    The episode fades ([PDR], [WIKI], [HIST]).


3. CONTEXT AND THEORIES (none is proven)
----------------------------------------
Context        Strasbourg had suffered bad harvests and famine, with diseases including smallpox and
               syphilis ([WAL08], [BRIT], [PDR]: "a string of bad harvests, political instability, and the
               arrival of syphilis").
Belief at the  Many people believed Saint Vitus could punish sinners by making them dance. The clergy and
time           many townspeople read the outbreak as a saint's curse ([WAL08], [WALL], [WIKI]).
Modern theory  Mass psychogenic illness, a stress-induced mass reaction shaped by belief in the curse. This is
1              Waller's argument, and Britannica calls it the most widely accepted ([WALL], [BRIT], [PDR]).
Modern theory  Ergot poisoning, from a toxic fungus on damp rye. It is often proposed but widely doubted:
2              ergot restricts blood flow, which makes days of dancing unlikely. Waller says "this theory
               does not seem tenable" ([WALL], [PDR], [WIKI]).
Other          A deliberate religious cult or ritual has also been suggested ([WIKI], [HIST]).

5. CONFIRMED vs UNCERTAIN
-------------------------
Well supported: the place and year; that it began with one woman in July; that it spread to dozens; the
physicians' "hot blood" verdict; the halls, stage and musicians; the ban that followed; the pilgrimage to
the shrine of Saint Vitus at Saverne; and that it ended by early September.

Uncertain (handled with hedged wording or left out):
- How many took part. Estimates run from about 50 to about 400; the narration says only "some accounts
  say hundreds".
- Deaths. Some modern retellings say "up to 15 a day", but according to [WIKI] the Strasbourg sources of
  the time do not record deaths, and the sources Waller cites that do mention deaths are later accounts.
  Waller himself writes that the chronicles suggest some died of exhaustion ([WALE]). Because the evidence
  conflicts, the video gives no death count and does not show anyone dying.
- The first dancer's name (Frau Troffea), the exact start date and her husband's role. All come from
  later accounts and are not used.
- The cause. Unknown; each theory is labelled as a belief or a theory, and ergot is labelled disputed.


6. ARTISTIC RECONSTRUCTION (labelled on screen as "AN ILLUSTRATED RECONSTRUCTION")
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Every face, person, costume, street and room is invented. The clothing follows the general look of
  the Upper Rhine around 1500-1520 (linen headcloths and long gowns for women; doublets, hose, broad
  flat caps and fur-trimmed gowns for men; physicians in long robes holding a urine flask, the usual
  symbol of their profession in pictures of the period). These are not portraits of real people.
- The council chamber, the stage scene and the procession are reconstructions of reported events. No
  dialogue is spoken or shown, and the papers on the council table carry no readable text.
- The city plan in scene 6 is a stylised bird's-eye view (the Ill river around the old island, the
  cathedral with its single north spire, finished in 1439). It is not a survey map.
- The shrine is drawn as a small chapel in wooded hills. Its real appearance in 1518 is not shown.
- The three theory panels are diagrams of ideas, not depictions of events.


7. FACTS CUT FOR TIME
---------------------
- Paracelsus, who visited later, blamed the first dancer's state of mind.
- Strasbourg was then a Free Imperial City of the Holy Roman Empire.
- Earlier outbreaks of dancing: Kolbigk (1021), Erfurt (1247), and the 1374 outbreaks along the Rhine.
- Strong men were reportedly paid to hold dancers upright.
- The red shoes, crosses and holy water used at the shrine.
